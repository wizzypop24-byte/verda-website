Magnesium forms, explained

28 October 2026 · 6 min read

Two bottles can both say “magnesium” and behave completely differently. The form — the compound the mineral is bound to — decides how much you actually absorb and how your stomach reacts.

The label usually hides the important number

A bottle that says “magnesium glycinate 1,000 mg” is not giving you 1,000 mg of magnesium. It is giving you 1,000 mg of the compound, of which only about 14% is elemental magnesium.

What matters is the elemental amount: the magnesium your body can actually use. A good label prints both, for example “magnesium glycinate providing 200 mg elemental magnesium”.

Glycinate: the gentle option

Magnesium bound to glycine is chelated, which means the mineral is wrapped in an amino acid. Many people tolerate it better than oxide and report fewer loose stools.

Because the compound is bulky, you need a larger capsule to reach a meaningful dose. That is why glycinate products often look expensive per gram.

Citrate: absorbable and slightly laxative

Magnesium citrate is well absorbed and is common in powders. At higher doses it draws water into the bowel, which is useful if you are constipated and unhelpful if you are not.

It is a reasonable choice for occasional use, but check the elemental dose before comparing prices.

Oxide: cheap, but mostly unabsorbed

Magnesium oxide contains a high percentage of elemental magnesium by weight, which makes it cheap. The problem is absorption: much of it passes through the gut unabsorbed.

It is not useless — it is used as a short-term laxative — but it is a poor choice if the goal is a daily magnesium supplement.

How to compare two bottles

1. Find the elemental magnesium per serving, not the compound weight.

2. Check the form: glycinate, citrate, malate, taurate, oxide.

3. Check the serving size: two capsules may be one serving.

4. Ignore “500 mg” on the front if the back says the serving is two capsules of a compound.

Bottom line

Form changes absorption and tolerance. Glycinate is a sensible default for daily use; citrate suits people who want a looser stool; oxide is best avoided for supplementation.

Whichever you choose, compare the elemental dose and the number of capsules per serving.
