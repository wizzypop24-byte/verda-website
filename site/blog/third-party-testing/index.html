What third-party testing checks

28 October 2026 · 5 min read

“Third-party tested” is printed on almost every supplement box. The useful question is: tested for what, by whom, and against which specification?

What a certificate of analysis is

A certificate of analysis, or CoA, is the lab's record of a specific batch. It should name the product, the batch number, the laboratory, the test methods and the result of each test.

If a brand cannot show a CoA for the batch you bought, “tested” is a claim rather than a fact.

Identity and potency

Identity testing checks that the ingredient in the bottle is the ingredient on the label. Potency testing measures how much is present compared with the declared amount.

A result within 90–110% of the label claim is common for vitamins and minerals. A result far below the claim suggests underdosing; far above suggests poor manufacturing control.

Heavy metals

Botanical and mineral ingredients can carry lead, arsenic, cadmium or mercury from soil or processing. Testing compares the result with a permitted limit.

A useful CoA lists the measured value and the limit, not just a tick box.

Microbiology

Capsules and powders can carry bacteria, yeast or mould. Microbial testing counts total aerobic bacteria, yeast and mould, and checks for specific pathogens such as E. coli and Salmonella.

This matters more for probiotics and botanicals than for a simple mineral tablet.

How to read the document

Match the batch number on the CoA to the batch number on your pack. Check the lab name and the test methods. Look for a pass or fail against a stated limit, not an unexplained “compliant”.

A CoA proves the batch met a specification. It does not prove the product works, and it is not a substitute for clinical evidence.

Bottom line

Ask for the batch-specific document. If the answer is a generic PDF with no batch number, keep asking.

Testing is a floor, not a finish line — but an untested product has no floor at all.
